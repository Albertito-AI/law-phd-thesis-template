# Design the legal argument

This guide provides questions for planning and reviewing a thesis. It does not prescribe a universal chapter order. Use the supplied example chapters as a scaffold, then organise the thesis around the problem and the evidence needed to answer it.

## Establish the contribution

Write a paragraph that identifies the legal problem, the precise question, the limits of existing scholarship, your proposed answer and the evidence needed to defend it. State the contribution at the level the research can support. A new classification, interpretation, comparison or evidential finding may be valuable without claiming to resolve an entire field.

Keep a short account of the strongest alternative explanation. Identify what evidence would require you to narrow or abandon your answer. Return to that account when revising the conclusion.

LSE's public law research-proposal guidance asks applicants to identify their question, relevant literature, methodology and case-selection criteria. Those prompts inform this planning approach; the application requirements themselves do not prescribe the structure of a completed thesis. [R1]

## Explain the method you actually use

| Approach | Questions to answer in the thesis |
| --- | --- |
| Doctrinal | Which sources have legal authority? Which version and jurisdiction apply? How are conflicting decisions, interpretive choices and gaps addressed? |
| Comparative | Why these jurisdictions or institutions? What is being compared? Which differences in legal function, language and institutional setting limit the comparison? |
| Socio-legal or empirical | How were participants, documents or observations selected? What permissions and ethics decisions apply? How do coding, missing observations and uncertainty affect the findings? |
| Historical | What period and archive define the inquiry? Which sources survive, which are absent, and how do later concepts affect interpretation? |
| Normative | Which evaluative premises are adopted? Why are they appropriate, and how does the conclusion change under a defensible competing premise? |

Methods may be combined. Explain the contribution and limits of each rather than presenting a list of labels. A doctrinal account of what a rule requires does not, by itself, establish how frequently people comply with it.

## Keep claims and authorities connected

Use [claim-evidence.csv](../examples/claim-evidence.csv) as an optional research record. For each consequential proposition, record its source, exact pinpoint, legal status or version, qualification, contrary authority and verification status. The example rows are workflow demonstrations, not completed research findings.

Read the cited material itself. Check that a case supports the proposition for which it is cited, that the relied-on passage belongs to the relevant judgment, and that a later decision or amendment has not changed the position. An authentic URL confirms neither the correctness of a proposition nor the accuracy of a pinpoint.

Distinguish a quotation, a paraphrase, an inference and a recommendation. Record a legal cut-off where the thesis depends on the state of the law. Preserve a route from translated text to the original and state who produced the translation.

Use [comparative-matrix.csv](../examples/comparative-matrix.csv) to make the comparison explicit. Record the common question before collecting jurisdiction-specific answers. Leave an unverified cell marked as such; absence of located evidence is not proof of absence.

## Make chapters work together

Each substantive chapter should identify the question it answers, develop the necessary analysis and explain what follows for the thesis. Use cross-references to connect arguments already established elsewhere. The conclusion should state the answer, its evidential basis, its limits and the consequences that follow.

For a thesis based on articles, add an integrating introduction and synthesis. Explain overlapping material, co-authorship, the contribution of each article and any differences between published and thesis versions. Groningen's publication-based route expressly requires coherence and a connecting introduction and conclusion. [R2]

## Plan word counts and review

Allocate space according to argumentative importance. Keep main text, footnotes, appendices and bibliography distinguishable in word-count reports because universities count them differently. Oxford's inspected 2025/26 law regulations, for example, include footnotes and appendices in the DPhil limit while excluding specified preliminary and reference material. This is a versioned example, not a setting that governs every student. [R3]

Use supervisor comments to maintain a decision log: issue, proposed response, change made, remaining disagreement and supporting evidence. Before submission, ask a reader to challenge the thesis's central inference, source selection and strongest counterargument.
